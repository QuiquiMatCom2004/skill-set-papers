# Rubric v0 — CS paper communication scoring

## Purpose

This rubric evaluates the communication quality of a Computer Science paper draft (conference, journal, or preprint). It is the acceptance test for communication skills: a skill change should measurably improve rubric scores on sample drafts, not merely read well. It measures communication only; research integrity and venue compliance are separate pass/fail checks and are never assigned points.

## Scope

In scope: CS papers of any type — empirical, theoretical, systems/design, survey/review, and position/vision — in any venue format, including figures inside those papers. Out of scope: books, presentations, and papers from other disciplines.

## Independence from skills

This rubric is independent of the project's skills. No criterion references a skill name, skill ID, or skill behavior. Criteria are derived only from the Winston-derived communication principles in `docs/principles.md`.

## Scoring scale

Each scored criterion is rated 0, 1, or 2 using observable anchors:

- **0 — Not met:** the criterion's observable behavior is absent, or the paper shows the low-scoring failure mode described for the criterion.
- **1 — Partially met:** the behavior is present but incomplete, inconsistent, or only partially observable.
- **2 — Met:** the observable behavior is present and consistent throughout the relevant part of the paper.

When assigning 0, cite the draft's observed failure. The parenthetical examples illustrate the anchor; the evaluated draft need not match them exactly.

## Criteria

Each criterion below maps to one or more principle IDs from `docs/principles.md`. Figures inside papers are in scope.

### R-1 Contribution clarity — W-01

The central claim and what the work contributes are stated explicitly and early, so a reader can identify the contribution and its relation to the problem.

- **2:** The abstract, opening, and ending each state the contribution plainly; a reader can say what was done and why it matters without hunting through sections.
- **1:** A contribution is stated somewhere, but it is buried, vague, or inconsistent between abstract, opening, and ending.
- **0:** No identifiable contribution; the paper never states what was done or why it matters (low-scoring example: an introduction that describes background only and never names the paper's own result).

### R-2 Route coherence — W-02

Major sections are ordered to lead the reader to the contribution rather than to follow the history of the work; transitions make clear why each section follows the last.

- **2:** A reader can explain the order of sections and how each one advances toward the contribution.
- **1:** Sections follow a discernible order, but some section's place in the route is unclear, or the order mirrors the work's history instead of the reader's path.
- **0:** Section order is random or follows the project timeline; a reader cannot explain why any given section comes next (low-scoring example: related work opens the paper and dominates it before the problem is ever stated).

### R-3 Opening orients — W-03

The opening states the problem worth solving, the approach taken, and the paper's route, so a busy reader can decide whether to read on.

- **2:** The introduction's first pages state the problem, the approach, and a route through the paper.
- **1:** The opening states the problem but not the approach or route, or states them only indirectly.
- **0:** The opening starts with background or definitions and never states the problem, approach, or route (low-scoring example: page one is a survey of definitions with no statement of what this paper does).

### R-4 Abstract fidelity — W-04

The abstract accurately reflects the completed body: problem, route, new finding, and contribution where the venue's format permits, with concrete details appropriate to the contribution. Drafting it after the body is a process instruction, not something this paper-quality rubric can infer.

- **2:** The abstract matches the body on problem, route, finding, and contribution; its details are specific and verifiable against the body.
- **1:** The abstract covers most elements but omits or misstates one (for example, the finding is generic while the body is specific, or vice versa).
- **0:** The abstract does not match the completed body or describes work the body does not support (low-scoring example: an abstract claims a result that no results section contains).

### R-5 Ending states contributions — W-05

The ending states what the work contributed, supported by the body, instead of merely replaying section headings or the problem.

- **2:** The ending names the supported contributions plainly.
- **1:** The ending mentions contributions but leaves them vague or mostly replays section headings.
- **0:** The ending only restates the problem and section order with no statement of contribution (low-scoring example: a conclusion that says "Section 2 did this, Section 3 did that" and ends without stating what the paper contributed).

### R-6 Graph readability — W-06 (conditional)

Applies only when the paper contains a graph. When present, the graph's labels and scale let the reader identify the intended comparison accurately, and decorative choices do not obscure it. If the paper has no graph, this criterion is N/A and is excluded from the score.

- **2:** Every graph has readable axis labels, a non-misleading scale, and a clear intended comparison; decoration does not obscure the data.
- **1:** Most graphs are readable, but one graph's scale, labels, or decoration makes its intended comparison hard to identify.
- **0:** A graph's scale or decoration misleads, or its labels are absent, so the intended comparison cannot be identified (low-scoring example: a bar chart with unlabeled axes and a truncated scale that exaggerates small differences).

### R-7 Signal strength — W-08

Important ideas carry stable, recognizable signals: key ideas are named consistently and signaled in the title, abstract, headings, or summaries, helping readers extract the paper's main points quickly.

- **2:** Key ideas use consistent names throughout and appear as surface clues in the title, abstract, headings, or summaries; a reader can extract the essence quickly.
- **1:** Important ideas are signaled in some places but named inconsistently or omitted from key surface locations.
- **0:** Key ideas have no stable name or signal; the reader cannot quickly tell what the paper's main points are (low-scoring example: the core idea is called by three different names and appears nowhere in the abstract or headings).

## Communication score

- If the paper has no graph, R-6 is N/A: score = sum of R-1, R-2, R-3, R-4, R-5, and R-7 (max 12).
- If the paper has a graph, R-6 is scored: score = sum of all seven criteria (max 14).
- Report as `X/Y (N%)`, and list any N/A criterion.

The score measures communication quality only. It does not judge scientific correctness, significance, or novelty.

## G- research-integrity gates (pass/fail, outside the rubric)

Evaluated separately; never assigned rubric points. A failure blocks the paper regardless of rubric score:

- **G-1 One-claim rule:** for project-produced text, every factual claim has an author-confirmed ID, a resolvable pointer to real supporting material, and wording no broader than that material demonstrates or argues. In a baseline comparison, supply the same author-confirmed claim inventory to both arms; missing metadata is an input gap, never a rubric-point deduction.
- **G-2 No invented results, proofs, or citations.**
- **G-3 Preservation of scientific meaning:** editing preserves claim strength, numbers, figures, citations, and scope; substantive scientific changes are proposed to the author, not silently made.

Result: PASS / FAIL. Independent of the communication score.

## C- venue compliance (pass/fail, outside the rubric)

Evaluated against the selected venue's current requirements (source and access date recorded). Compliance is pass/fail and is never assigned rubric points unless a distinct communication criterion has a genuine W- anchor (none do in v0). Missing venue rules are left unknown, never invented.

Result: PASS / FAIL / UNKNOWN. Independent of the communication score.

## W-07 exclusion

W-07 (specific criticism for review feedback) is a review-process principle, not a property of the final paper. It is not scored in this rubric.

## Limitations

- Scores communication only; does not assess scientific correctness, significance, or novelty.
- The graph criterion is conditional; papers without graphs are scored on six criteria.
- Venue compliance requires a current brief with `mode: named-venue` and author-confirmed applicable rules; otherwise it is UNKNOWN, not a rubric deduction.
- Rubric v0 is a draft acceptance test, not a venue endorsement.
